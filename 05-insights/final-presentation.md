## Slide 1 · Product Strategy

### Fable's real competitor is the user deciding they are done

**The diagnosis behind the headline.** Fable works. Adults resolve the anxiety episode they came for in 90 to 120 days, conclude they are finished, and stop opening the app. 4.2M users, growth flat. You cannot fix that with engagement mechanics, because the mechanics would be arguing with a user who is right.

**Playing to Win.**

| Choice | Our answer |
|---|---|
| Winning aspiration | The first thing you talk to when something feels off, because it has known you for two years. |
| Where to play | Existing users, plus recent graduates, meaning people who got better and stopped using it, 25 to 45, US English first. Everyday wellbeing, not just acute episodes. Not clinical care, not general assistance. |
| How to win | Compounding personal context inside a clinically grounded companion. After a year, switching means starting over with a stranger. |
| Capabilities | Build the memory engine and clinical conversation design. Partner for the model, clinical advisors and crisis escalation. World-class at safety. |
| Management systems | Felt-understood score and 26-week retention. Weekly safety review with halt authority, monthly clinical audit, safety-eval ship gate. |

**The hard no.** No attachment optimization, friendship or romance simulation, time-in-app targets, or dependency mechanics. Attachment destroys the clinical trust that is our only durable moat.

**OKRs.** KR1, share of users still here at 26 weeks: 12% to 30%. KR2, share of conversations people rate 4 or better on "did this feel understood": 38% to 65%, clinically audited. KR3, users who got better and left, coming back for a second conversation: 5% to 18%.

> Say: the growth wall is caused by the product working, so the strategy has to change what the product is, not how sticky it is. If asked how we win against ChatGPT: it starts every mental-health conversation cold, and Calm and Headspace hold attention rather than history.

## Slide 2 · Outcome Roadmap

### Three Rocks, four cuts, and the cuts are where the strategy is

**Now, committed.** The three Rocks, meaning the bets that have to land or the strategy does not.

- **Memory-grounded check-ins:** the thing competitors cannot copy. Drives KR2. Memory & Personalization, Conversation Design.
- **Crisis mode:** what makes it safe to run this at all, and the safe way back for someone who left. Drives KR3. Safety & Clinical, Platform.
- **Sub-2-second load:** if it takes four seconds to open at 3am, the promise is already broken. Drives KR1. Platform, Engineering.

**Performance as a Rock, deliberately.** Speed usually gets filed as polish, not strategy. Here it is a Rock because the product's whole promise is being the first thing you reach for, and nothing that takes four seconds is the first thing you reach for.

**What we cut.**

- **Daily streaks:** the hard no in feature form.
- **Content library expansion:** invests further in the very thing users finish with.
- **Social sharing:** mental-health progress is not social currency.
- **Fable for Teams:** employer reporting conflicts with the hard no and the privacy promise. Revisit after KR2.

**Later, bets not commitments.** Voice, web and watch surfaces. Spanish and Portuguese markets. A supporter track for the people around a struggling user, plus clinician-ready export.

**Sequencing the Next queue.** WSJF, which ranks work by value and urgency divided by effort, puts the pricing pilot top of the queue at 6.00, mostly because it removes risk. Price gate set at 30 November rather than waiting for the retention read.

> Say: a roadmap that does not make clear what is not being built is not a roadmap. Fable for Teams is the expensive cut, and it is marked revisit after KR2 rather than never.

## Slide 3 · Team Charter

### A hard no only works if a ticket can be tested against it

**What we own.** We own the companion relationship end to end. A shared platform with product teams on top. Memory, conversation design and safety are built once, centrally; three PMs own outcomes that use them. We optimize for coherence and accept slower velocity in the shared layer, because a companion that behaves like two products is not a companion. The rule that settles the overlap: whoever owns the screen decides where and when a check-in appears, and whoever owns the check-in decides what it says.

**How we decide.** Metric owner decides. Capability owner can veto. Safety and the hard no stop anything outright. Not overridable. No cross-team conflict stays open more than three business days.

**The seam this charter had to close.** Retention's fastest levers and the hard no look identical in a ticket. Both are a notification. The line: a check-in is earned return if a user-specific signal triggers it, and manufactured dependency if elapsed time or a frequency target triggers it.

**What success looks like.** 26-week retention 12% to 30%. Felt-understood 4+ share 38% to 65%. Graduate second-conversation rate 5% to 18%. Zero unhandled safety incidents. Miss retention and felt-understood together and we replan the check-in roadmap before next quarter.

> Say: this is the module where I found out my strategy's hard no was unusable as written.

## Slide 4 · Financial Model

### Not a growth bet. A repair of unit economics sitting within cents of cost of capital

**Where Fable is today.** For every $1.00 we spend acquiring someone, we get $1.05 back, and it takes 25 months. That is breaking even, not earning. We need $9.57 a month per user just to break even, and we charge $9.99, so we clear the line by 42 cents. If people leave a little faster than assumed, breakeven rises to $10.20 and we lose 21 cents on each one. Nobody has measured which world we are in.

**What the bet does.** A $1.0M build takes that to $1.82 back on every $1.00, paying back in 10 months, worth about $11.6M a year. It drops the break-even point to $6.40 a month. The $3.59 of room above break-even is the real product, not the return.

**The ask.** $1.0M, six engineers for two quarters, gated on the felt-understood score hitting 55% by 31 December.

| Stage | LTV:CAC |
|---|---|
| Today | 1.05:1 |
| After the bet | 1.82:1 |
| Sustainable (needs roughly 59% retention, unreachable here) | 3:1 |

**Seven kill criteria. Here is one.** If felt-understood 4+ share has not reached 55% by 31 December 2026, we do not commit the Q1 2027 headcount: six engineers, about $375K per quarter, return to crisis mode. The gate that matters most checks, at month 12, how long people actually stay once they pass six months, which carries 74% of the value.

> Say: the case got stronger when it stopped claiming to be a growth story. If asked why 3:1 is not the bar, it would take roughly 59% 26-week retention, which this mechanism cannot reach, so judge it on the room gained above break-even instead.

## Slide 5 · Individual Insights

### A strategy is only as real as the number that would end it

**Friction.** The hard no took two minutes to write and four modules to make usable. Module 2 made me cut real revenue. Module 3 showed the rule could not be applied to a ticket. Module 4 caught me about to concede the loophole myself: time since last open is a user-specific signal.

**Learning: good economics, wrong bet.** Good unit economics don't make a bet worth funding. The case I reviewed had better numbers than mine. Still a no. The ceiling was $414K against a 4.2M-user business.

**Learning: the wrong assumption scared me.** The assumption I feared wasn't the one carrying the case. Being 30% wrong about how strongly feeling understood drives retention still leaves the bet worth funding. But how long people stay once they pass six months, a number nobody has measured, carries 74% of the value.

**The aha.** The strategy felt done when all the pieces lined up, but nothing in it could be proven wrong. Writing kill criteria changed that. Judgment only travels if someone who isn't me can act on it.

> Say: close on this. The hard no was a value until I wrote the test. The strategy was an opinion until I wrote the number that would make me stop.
